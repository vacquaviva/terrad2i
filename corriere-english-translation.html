Viviana Acquaviva, who left astrophysics to save the oceans: “In the US, they pay me a year’s salary to change careers”

By Eleonora Chioda

This is an English translation of an article originally published by Corriere della Sera on September 24, 2026.

In the United States, there is a fellowship for people who, in the middle of their careers, want to stop doing something they are already very good at and start again in another field. It is called the Pivot Fellowship and is funded by the Simons Foundation. If you win one, it pays your salary for a full year, provided you do none of your previous work during that time. Viviana Acquaviva was among the first seven researchers to receive it.

A physicist and astrophysicist, she is a full professor at the City University of New York, the largest public university in the Northeast, with nearly 250,000 students. Three years ago, she left astrophysics to work in climate science, from improving global climate models to measuring how much carbon the ocean can absorb.

Would you do it?

“There is a lot to gain from giving up control, at least for a while. I gave up being the expert. That forces me to place more trust in others, especially the younger generations. It is a new way of doing science.”

Change is nothing unusual for her. Back in 2011, while studying stars, Acquaviva began working on machine learning, before it became fashionable. During the pandemic, she wrote a book about artificial intelligence, physics and astronomy. Reading geophysics papers, she discovered that the field fascinated her.

“Then I saw an announcement on Twitter for a lab that had opened at Columbia University in 2021, called LEAP—Learning the Earth with AI and Physics. I wrote to the center’s directors and, trying not to sound completely crazy, put myself forward for the fellowship. They welcomed me: the center’s co-director and director became my mentors. Then the Simons Foundation gave me the opportunity to apply. And because the fellowship pays 100% of your salary for a year, the university agreed.”

Acquaviva subsequently won a $1.8 million grant to build a new research group at her university. Making the leap is frightening. “But fear can be an ally: you don’t have to go around it; you can go through it.”

Today, she spends her days “massaging” data to extract information and answer questions that no one can quantify with certainty—for example, how much carbon the oceans absorb. “We have developed new mathematical metrics to extract more meaningful information than the metrics normally used. The available data are very sparse: they come mainly from research ships, and there is much more coverage in the Northern Hemisphere—where there is more land and more shipping traffic—and in summer, when conditions are more favorable. The challenge is to work out how to fill the gaps in the data and plan where new observations would be most useful.”

How is it measured?

“We measure the concentration of carbon or other relevant elements at the ocean surface. The most precise measurements are made by research ships, but floats have been developed more recently. These are instruments that remain in one area while moving up and down through the water. They are cheaper than ships and can be deployed in more places, but they are less precise. We are trying to use these two sources of data together.”

The ocean plays a major role in the global carbon cycle because it absorbs carbon. “To understand what will happen with global warming, we need to understand that balance. We know—though not even that well—how much we emit. One missing piece is understanding how much the ocean absorbs and how that changes over time. With more carbon in the atmosphere, the ocean absorbs more, but at some point its capacity could become saturated.”

From Lecce to New York

Acquaviva is from Lecce. She attended a science-focused high school, earned a physics degree in Pisa and completed a doctorate in astrophysics at SISSA in Trieste. “At that point I received several postdoc offers, and the best was in the United States: ten months at the University of Pennsylvania, then a little more than two years at Princeton. I left thinking, ‘I’ll go for a few years, then come back.’ But in 2012 I got a faculty position in New York, and I’ve now been in the United States for twenty years.”

She sometimes dreams of returning. “But it is hard to imagine now. My husband is Egyptian, and our daughter was born here. This is a wonderful place, full of opportunities, but it has many problems. The biggest one for me? It does not treat vulnerable people well—the young or the elderly. Someone who lives in a neighborhood with poor public schools is disadvantaged from elementary school onward, because investment in schools depends on local taxes. New York is a Democratic state with a strong tradition of education, but that is not true everywhere.

“On the other hand, if you want to pursue something, there are opportunities here that are hard to imagine elsewhere. The fellowship I won is a high-risk program that gives someone a chance based on their potential, not their past achievements. I had never worked on climate, but I love learning. I feel like a perpetual student, and I am fortunate to be able to do that with the security of an established career.”

With her group, she develops mathematical models of climate data.

“We try to understand, from current conditions, what will happen to temperature, precipitation and so on over the medium and long term. For years, this was done by solving the differential equations of physical systems. Today we also use machine learning, which trains predictive models on past data. But the problem is that the climate is a continuously evolving system, and past data alone are not enough. It takes a little more creativity to work out how to extrapolate to new conditions.”

I meet her at City Tech in Brooklyn, in her laboratory at the university. She works with a group of five scientists, including oceanographers and computer scientists; two are young Italian researchers. I ask how the planet is doing. I expect her to say “badly,” so her answer catches me off guard.

“The planet is fine; we’re the ones who aren’t.” She jokes, then turns serious. “I don’t like the idea of ‘saving the planet.’ It changes course, readjusts and has an extraordinary ability to do so—just not on the timescale we need. We are not going to end up like Venus.

“But we scientists have missed a major opportunity to communicate this reality. We want to be accurate at all costs, and sometimes that comes at the expense of communicating effectively. If we say we will exceed the Paris Agreement’s target of one and a half degrees of global warming, someone might think, ‘Well, it’ll be a little warmer; that doesn’t sound so bad.’ The problem is that floods and fires become more frequent, and entire areas that were once farmable become impossible to cultivate. I wouldn’t declare disaster simply because we miss the one-and-a-half-degree target. It isn’t a magic number beyond which ‘there’s no point anymore.’ Reducing warming is still better than doing nothing.”

What difficulties has she faced?

“The same ones all women face. When I applied to become an associate professor, my daughter was six months old. Someone said to me, ‘You want to apply so soon? Shouldn’t you be at home worrying about the baby?’—as though I wasn’t worrying about her while I was at the office breastfeeding. There are still few women in physics. But what caused me the most difficulty was my identity as an immigrant: communicating in a language that isn’t your own, following teleconferences in English without being able to see people’s faces—before Zoom—and the culture shock of figuring out how things work in the United States. The culture here is very competitive.

“At Princeton, my impression was that everyone knew exactly where they were headed professionally. I, on the other hand, felt rather lost. It was hard, but I’m glad I spent time looking for the right path for me. The conventional route to academic success is to become exceptionally good at one thing and keep studying it with your students. I’m not very good at capitalizing on that: I like change, and I have no qualms about asking for help. I think helping one another is a fundamental part of being human, one we too often undervalue at work. Of course, you have to let go of a little ego. As a full professor, I was used to going to conferences because I had been invited to give a talk. In the new field, especially at the beginning, I was often the one asking. For me, the reward is in the journey. Today I get to work every day with new people who are young, brilliant and enthusiastic.”

Acquaviva also served on the AI committee for Italy’s 2024–2026 national strategy.

“I don’t think anyone really knows how the field of artificial intelligence will evolve. What interests me most is giving younger researchers the tools to navigate this transition. So this year I began teaching a course on large language models for doctoral students. I think it is important for us as academics not to underestimate the impact of these new technologies and to take part both in their development and in their governance.”

What have you learned during your career that might be useful to the rest of us?

“As children, we are taught to do things well. And that’s absolutely fine. But in academia, we often want things to be the best they can possibly be, and that creates unreasonable pressure. Along the way, I’ve learned that what matters is not whether a piece of work is perfect, but whether it is useful. I wrote a textbook on machine learning, and I was extremely intimidated. Then I told myself: it doesn’t have to be the best book possible, but it can certainly be a useful book. Since then, I’ve focused less on myself and more on how my research can be useful to others. If what I do can help even just five people understand something better, that’s fine. That’s enough.”
